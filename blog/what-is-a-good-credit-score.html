What is a Good Credit Score? Complete Guide

August 2, 2026 8 min read

Your credit score is one of the most important numbers in your financial life — it affects whether you can get a loan, what interest rate you'll pay, and sometimes even whether you can rent an apartment. Yet most people don't fully understand how it works or what actually influences it.

What is a Credit Score?

A credit score is a three-digit number that represents your creditworthiness — essentially, how likely you are to repay borrowed money based on your past financial behavior. Lenders use it to quickly assess the risk of lending to you. The higher the score, the lower the perceived risk, and the better the loan terms you're likely to receive.

Credit scores are calculated by credit bureaus using mathematical models that analyze your credit history. The most widely used model in many countries is the FICO score, which ranges from 300 to 850.

Credit Score Ranges Explained

  • 300–579 — Poor: Significantly below average. Most lenders will reject applications or offer very high interest rates. May require a secured credit card or co-signer to begin rebuilding.
  • 580–669 — Fair: Below average. Some lenders will approve loans but at higher rates. Room for significant improvement with consistent positive habits.
  • 670–739 — Good: Near or slightly above average. Most lenders will approve applications, and you'll qualify for reasonably competitive rates.
  • 740–799 — Very Good: Above average. You'll qualify for better-than-average rates from most lenders and have strong approval odds.
  • 800–850 — Exceptional: Well above average. You'll qualify for the best available rates and terms from virtually any lender.

What Makes Up Your Credit Score?

The FICO model breaks down credit scores into five factors, each weighted differently:

  • Payment History (35%) — The single most important factor. Whether you pay bills on time consistently. Even one missed payment can significantly lower your score.
  • Amounts Owed / Credit Utilization (30%) — How much of your available credit you're using. Using more than 30% of your credit limit on any card tends to hurt your score; under 10% is ideal.
  • Length of Credit History (15%) — How long you've had credit accounts. Older accounts and a longer average account age generally help your score.
  • Credit Mix (10%) — Having a variety of credit types (credit cards, installment loans, mortgages) can help, though this is less important than the top two factors.
  • New Credit (10%) — Applying for multiple new credit accounts in a short period can temporarily lower your score, as each hard inquiry signals potential financial stress.

Why Your Credit Score Matters

  • Loan approval and interest rates — A higher score means better rates. The difference between a good and poor score can mean paying thousands more in interest over a loan's lifetime.
  • Credit card approvals — Better scores unlock better cards with lower rates, higher limits, and better rewards.
  • Renting property — Many landlords check credit scores as part of the rental application process.
  • Employment — Some employers in financial or sensitive roles check credit history as part of background screening.
  • Insurance premiums — In some countries, insurers use credit-based insurance scores to help determine premiums.

How to Improve Your Credit Score

Pay every bill on time, every time. Since payment history is 35% of your score, this is the single most impactful thing you can do. Set up automatic payments to never accidentally miss a due date.

Reduce credit card balances. Try to keep utilization below 30% on each card — ideally below 10%. Paying down existing balances is one of the fastest ways to improve your score.

Don't close old accounts. Closing old credit cards reduces your available credit (increasing utilization) and shortens your average credit history length — both can hurt your score, even if the card has a zero balance.

Limit new credit applications. Only apply for new credit when you genuinely need it. Multiple applications in a short period signal financial stress to lenders.

Check your credit report for errors. Mistakes on credit reports are more common than people realize — incorrect late payments, accounts that don't belong to you, or outdated negative information can all unfairly lower your score. Dispute any errors you find with the credit bureau.

How Long Does It Take to Improve?

Credit score improvement is not instant — it's a reflection of consistent behavior over time. Small positive changes (like paying down a card balance) can show results within 1-2 months. Recovering from a significant negative event like a missed payment or default typically takes 12-24 months of consistent positive behavior to meaningfully offset.

The most important mindset shift is treating your credit score as a long-term asset to maintain, not a short-term number to manipulate. Consistent, simple habits maintained over years produce the best results.
